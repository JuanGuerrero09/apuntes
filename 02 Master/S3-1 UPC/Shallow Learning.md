# Unit 1: Introduction to AI and Machine Learning

### 

**Lesson 1.1 - Introduction to AI**

This lesson covers the foundational definition of Artificial Intelligence (AI), exploring its goal of creating machines for intelligent tasks and contrasting it with common, incorrect notions such as the pursuit of human-like consciousness. It explains the difference between symbolic and subsymbolic (Machine Learning) approaches. Furthermore, it introduces key philosophical categorizations, including the distinction between Weak AI and Strong AI, and emphasizes the truly multidisciplinary nature of AI, which draws heavily from fields like Philosophy, Mathematics, and Neuroscience.

Artificial Intelligence is a multidisciplinary field with the goal of creating machines which can solve complex (i.e. intelligent) tasks.

Here are some tasks which [Hofstadter 1979] considered intelligent and, therefore, interesting challenges for the field of AI:

- Playing games, like chess
- Translation
- Speaking

AI is sometimes confused with machine learning because of the popularity of ML models. However, ML is a subfield of AI, like painting or poetry are subfields of the arts.

There are two main paradigms in AI: **symbolic approaches**, which operate on explicit rules and logic, and **subsymbolic approaches**, which learn patterns from data.

## 

**AI categorizations**

Since the definition of AI depends heavily on the definition of intelligence, in 1980, philosopher John Searle introduced a distinction between **weak AI and strong AI**.

- Weak: The idea that machines can act intelligently, and it is a pragmatic, mundane and achievable goal.
- Strong: The assertion that machines are actually producing intelligent thought.



### 

**Lesson 1.2 - Introduction to Machine Learning**

This lesson covers the core concept of Machine Learning (ML) as a subfield of AI that learns from data rather than explicit programming. It introduces the three classical ML subfields: Supervised, Unsupervised, and Reinforcement learning, as well as the emerging Self- supervised learning paradigm, focusing primarily on the first two. The lesson also explores various model families, including linear models, tree-based models, and neural networks, and clarifies the concepts of white box vs. black box models and the critical issue of biases and interpretability in ML systems.





### 

**Lesson 1.3 - Supervised Learning**

This lesson covers the core paradigm of Supervised Learning, details the fundamental distinction between regression and classification problems, and outlines the essential five-step process for training a model. It also clarifies critical practical concepts like structuring data into the X-y matrix, the importance of splitting data into training and test sets to prevent overfitting, and introduces an overview of typical model families used in the field.

### 

**Lesson 1.4 - Regression vs Classification**

This lesson covers the fundamental distinction between regression and classification, the two main types of supervised machine learning. It clarifies the different evaluation metrics used for each, including Mean Squared Error, accuracy, the confusion matrix, precision, recall, and the F1 score. Finally, it explains the key differences in encoding the target variable for each problem type, with a detailed look at why one-hot encoding is critical for multiclass problems.

### 

**Lesson 1.5 - Cross Validation**

This lesson introduces Cross Validation, which is an advanced training and testing technique.
