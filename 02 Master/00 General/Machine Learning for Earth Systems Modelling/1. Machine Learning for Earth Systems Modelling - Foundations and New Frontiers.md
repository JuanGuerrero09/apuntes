### Classes of machine learning

- Supervised Learning: A type of machine learning where models are trained using labelled examples that show both the input data and the correct output. 
	- Regression: A machine learning task that predicts a continuous numerical value, such as temperature, wind speed, or precipitation amount.
	- Classification: A machine learning task that assigns inputs to discrete categories or classes, such as predicting whether convection will occur or not.
- Unsupervised learning: A type of machine learning that discovers patterns or structure in data without using labelled outputs.
	- Find structure
	- Clustering: An unsupervised learning method that groups similar data points together based on their characteristics.
	- Dimension reduction: An unsupervised learning method that reduces the number of variables in a dataset while preserving the most important information or structure.
- Reinforcement learning: A learning approach where an agent improves its decisions through trial and error by receiving rewards or penalties from its environment.
- Neural Networks: Machine learning models inspired by the structure of the brain, consisting of interconnected layers of computational units that transform input data into predictions.
- Deep Learning: A class of machine learning methods based on large neural networks with many layers that can learn complex patterns directly from data.

Summary

The paper discusses the degree to which ML can represent complex processes that physics-based weather and climate models cannot resolve or whose physics are not fully known, such as clouds, more efficiently than traditional schemes.

### Artificial Intelligence in DestinE – The Explainer

The AI serves multiple purposes in DestinE. These roles range from processing vast amounts of environmental data, to improving the accuracy of climate simulations and to making the system more adaptive and responsive as new information becomes available.

AI in DestinE is organized around three main pillars:
- Software Development: AI in DestinE is part of software engineering that can handle massive datasets, integrate models, and ensure scalability across supercomputing platforms.
- Model Development: Machine learning models are central to improving predictions, refining simulations, and enabling adaptive systems that can learn from new environmental data.
- Ethical and Responsable AI: DestinE emphasizes transparency, fairness, and accountability to ensure that AI applications serve society responsibly and align with ethical standards.

Terms:
- Trainng: The process of adjusting a machine learning model’s parameters using data so that it learns to map inputs (e.g. atmospheric states) to outputs (e.g. future states).
- Loss Function: A measure of how different the model’s predictions are from the true values, which the training process aims to minimise.
- Gradient Descent: An optimisation method that iteratively updates model parameters in the direction that most reduces the loss function.
- MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron): A type of neural network composed of fully connected layers that maps input variables to outputs through a series of nonlinear transformations.
- Inference: The process of using a trained model to make predictions on new data, such as generating a weather forecast from current conditions.
- Roll-out: The repeated application of a model’s predictions as inputs to itself to simulate the evolution of a system over multiple time steps.
- Zero/One/Few-Shot Learning: Approaches where a model can perform a task with no, one, or only a few examples, by leveraging knowledge learned from other data or tasks.

### A very short introduction to AIFS

AIFS: AI Forecasting System of ECMWF

How does the AIFS work? The AIFS uses the same initial conditions as ECMWF’s traditional Integrated Forecasting System (IFS), combining a short-term forecast with millions of quality-controlled observations. Every six hours, these initial conditions are fed into the machine-learning model, which has been trained on historical weather evolution to predict how the atmosphere will develop over the coming days.

### Rapid revolution of ML activities at ECMWF

New tools and infrastructure have been developed in ECMWF:

- **Anemoi open-source framework**: The Anemoi framework is an open-source platform developed to support machine learning for weather and climate prediction. It provides tools for training, evaluating, and running AI forecasting models using large Earth-system datasets.
- Expanded GPU computing resources: Better computing resources allow for shorter model training times, faster prediction times, or higher-resolution simulations, leading to more accurate predictions.
- Open datasets: More freely accessible meteorological datasets around the world increase the training pool for machine-learning models, helping to improve their accuracy and performance.
- Training programmes: Training programmes help researchers and meteorological services develop and integrate machine-learning approaches for weather prediction in their work.

### In-depth reading material on the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act is the first-ever legal framework on AI, which addresses the risks of AI in four levels: unacceptable risk, high risk, limited risk and minimal risk. Please notice that these risks concern all AI use in the EU and is not limited to meteorology. Weather forecasts specifically are not seen as unacceptable or high risk, but downstream applications for different impact sectors might be determined as high risk.

### Unacceptable risk

All AI systems considered a clear threat to the safety, livelihoods and rights of people are banned. The **AI Act prohibits eight practices**, namely:

1. harmful AI-based manipulation and deception
2. harmful AI-based exploitation of vulnerabilities
3. social scoring
4. Individual criminal offence risk assessment or prediction
5. untargeted scraping of the internet or CCTV material to create or expand facial recognition databases
6. emotion recognition in workplaces and education institutions
7. biometric categorisation to deduce certain protected characteristics
8. real-time remote biometric identification for law enforcement purposes in publicly accessible spaces
### High risk

AI use cases that can pose serious risks to health, safety or fundamental rights are classified as high-risk. These **high-risk** use-cases include:

- AI safety components in critical infrastructures (e.g. transport), the failure of which could put the life and health of citizens at risk
- AI solutions used in education institutions, that may determine the access to education and course of someone’s professional life (e.g. scoring of exams)
- AI-based safety components of products (e.g. AI application in robot-assisted surgery)
- AI tools for employment, management of workers and access to self-employment (e.g. CV-sorting software for recruitment)
- Certain AI use-cases utilised to give access to essential private and public services (e.g. credit scoring denying citizens opportunity to obtain a loan)
- AI systems used for remote biometric identification, emotion recognition and biometric categorisation (e.g. AI system to retroactively identify a shoplifter)
- AI use-cases in law enforcement that may interfere with people’s fundamental rights (e.g. evaluation of the reliability of evidence)
- AI use-cases in migration, asylum and border control management (e.g. automated examination of visa applications)
- AI solutions used in the administration of justice and democratic processes (e.g. AI solutions to prepare court rulings)


# Module 5: Looking Ahead

### Webinar: Machine learning for Earth systems modelling – what we might expect over the next decade

- Model Paradigms:There are differing views on the future architecture of AI weather and climate models—some advocate for unified models, while others favour more specialised, task-specific systems. Do we move toward unified frameworks (so-called foundation models), or a modular ecosystem of specialised models?
- Data ecosystems: Training AI models demands large, well-structured and openly available Earth system data sets, both of observations and model outputs. How can we realistically move toward such an open, ML-ready data infrastructure?
- Infrastructure and software: The transition from traditional NWP to AI-driven modelling requires a fundamental redesign of software and computational infrastructure. How should the community rethink software development to fully leverage modern hardware and AI workflows?
- Scientific discovery: AI is often framed as a tool for faster forecasts, and much of its contemporary discussion is about building better models. But it may also ultimately enable totally new kinds of scientific inquiry, through large ensembles, improved diagnostics, tighter integration of observations and models, and better representations of physical phenomena yet to be understood. What new scientific questions become possible with these capabilities? How could AI change our approach to discovery in earth system science?
- Building trust: Much current trust in weather models comes from their explicit attempt to respect the laws of physics. And much early criticism of AI-based models is that they do not have to respect these laws as stringently, and that it is hard to interrogate them. So how do we make AI-based models better aligned with physics, and, ultimately, how do we build trust in these models?
