## Block A: Setting the Scene (Flood Risk Management)

### The Goals and Dilemmas of FRM

Flood Risk Management (FRM) involves the activities that enable societies to cope with flood hazards. It must be tailored toward both the society it protects and the physics/hazards of the system.

Planners face several core questions and dilemmas:

- What do we actually aim for?
    
- How safe is "safe enough," and what level of risk is acceptable?
    
- What does "society is able to cope" actually mean? Does it mean _everybody_ in society, or just some?
    
- Should measures be primarily _efficient_ (cost-effective) or _fair_ (equitable distribution of protection)?
    

### The Risk Concept

Risk is defined by the interaction of its components:

**`RISK = Vulnerability × Exposure`** _(Note: Hazard is implied as the external force interacting with these)._

### Flood Impact Classification

Impacts are classified along two axes: Tangible vs. Intangible, and Direct vs. Indirect.

||**Tangible (Monetary)**|**Intangible (Non-monetary)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Direct** (Immediate physical contact)|**Physical damage to assets:**<br><br>  <br><br>• Buildings<br><br>  <br><br>• Infrastructure<br><br>  <br><br>• Content/inventory|**Non-monetary damages:**<br><br>  <br><br>• Casualties & Health impacts<br><br>  <br><br>• Loss of cultural heritage|
|**Indirect** (Ripple effects)|**Ripple effects:**<br><br>  <br><br>• Business interruption<br><br>  <br><br>• Economic production (supply chains)<br><br>  <br><br>• Utility lifelines & Traffic interruptions|**Social effects:**<br><br>  <br><br>• Interruption of education<br><br>  <br><br>• Lower economic prosperity of the area|

### Cascading Risk

Disasters are rarely isolated events; they cause a domino effect.

- **Event Trees:** Used to explore different failure scenarios.
    
- **Process:** Choose a triggering event -> Imagine all possible subsequent events (the cascade) -> Every specific chain of events constitutes a "scenario."
    

## Block B: Introducing Uncertainty

### Certainty vs. Probabilistic Approaches

- **Certainty (Deterministic):** A situation where the outcome that will occur is known. The probability of the outcome is 100%.
    
- **Probabilistic (Risk-based):** A situation where all possible outcomes and their probability of occurrence are known, even if you don't know _which_ specific outcome will happen (uses probability distributions).
    
    - _Pros of Probabilistic Design:_ Straightforward to design/manage; uses resources effectively; lots of historical experience.
        
    - _The Catch:_ It works well under **stationarity** (the assumption that environmental conditions will remain relatively stable and predictable based on the past).
        

### Deep Uncertainty

Deep uncertainty exists when we **cannot** confidently assign probabilities to future hazards, exposure, vulnerability, or system responses. Because of this, planners can no longer rely purely on prediction-based risk analysis.

_(This concept is often visualized using the Rumsfeld matrix: Known-Knowns, Known-Unknowns, Unknown-Unknowns)._

## Block C: Uncertainty Drivers

### Types of Uncertainty

1. **Aleatory uncertainty:** Natural randomness or variability in a system. It _cannot_ be eliminated.
    
2. **Epistemic uncertainty:** A lack of knowledge or data. It _can_ be reduced through better research and modeling.
    
3. **Scenario uncertainty:** Uncertainty regarding future pathways and human decisions. It _cannot_ be predicted, only explored.
    

### Where Uncertainty Enters the FRM System

Uncertainty propagates through every step of an impact assessment:

- Hazard uncertainty
    
- Climate scenario uncertainty
    
- Exposure data uncertainty
    
- Vulnerability function uncertainty
    
- Socioeconomic pathway uncertainty
    
- Model structure uncertainty
    

### Specific Drivers of Uncertainty

**1. Urban Growth & Socioeconomics:**

- Uncertainties in growth rate and physical extent (impacts _Exposure_).
    
- Socioeconomic development and demographic shifts, like age distribution (impacts _Vulnerability_).
    
- Changes in asset composition (housing types, roads) and technological developments.
    
- The level of risk-sensitive planning implemented.
    
- Location and development of future critical infrastructure.
    

**2. Other Drivers:**

- **Asset Management:** Aging water infrastructure, increasing failure rates, and substandard performance of old systems.
    
- Socioeconomic and political development shifts.
    
- **Environmental Standards:** Rising legal requirements (e.g., EU Water Framework Directive).
    
- **Societal Expectations:** Increasing demands for urban liveability (e.g., requiring more green/blue spaces in cities).
    

## Block D: Scenarios

Because of deep uncertainty, we cannot predict the exact future. Instead, **scenarios help us explore uncertainty, not forecast exactly.** They allow planners to test how different strategies might perform across a range of plausible futures.

# Module 01: Resilience Concept

**Tags:** #Resilience #Adaptation #WaterManagement #FRM

## Defining Resilience

**Resilience (IPCC, 2014 Definition):** The capacity of social, economic, and environmental systems to cope with a hazardous event or trend or disturbance, responding or reorganizing in ways that maintain their essential function, identity, and structure, while also maintaining the capacity for adaptation, learning, and transformation.

### The Scope of Resilience

Resilience is a broadly used term, but it only becomes relevant and measurable when placed within a clear scope by asking four questions:

1. Resilience of **what system**?
    
2. To **what hazard** or disturbance?
    
3. **For whom**?
    
4. Over **what time scale**?
    

## Dimensions of Resilience

Resilience is multi-faceted and spans across several different domains of a system:

- **Physical / Infrastructural**
    
- **Ecological**
    
- **Social**
    
- **Institutional:** Organizations, rules, procedures, and formal arrangements.
    
- **Governance:** The entire decision-making and coordination system.
    
- **Financial**
    
- **Economic**
    

## Types of Resilience: Engineering vs. Ecological

- **Engineering Resilience:** Focuses on the system’s return to the exact same equilibrium it had prior to the shock or disturbance.
    
- **Ecological Resilience:** Focuses on dynamic equilibria in complex interactive systems (strange attractors). It is the system's ability to absorb change without fundamentally changing its stage or overarching function.
    

## System Performance Over Time

When a system undergoes a shock, its performance can be measured in four phases:

1. **Resistance:** How much the system's performance drops initially.
    
2. **Absorption / Coping:** How the system continues functioning during the actual disruption.
    
3. **Recovery:** How fast the system rebounds after the shock.
    
4. **Adaptation:** How the system adjusts to perform better the next time a shock occurs.
    

## Adaptation Under Changing Conditions

Adaptation is not a single concept; it changes based on the type of future condition the system is facing:

|**Condition Shift**|**Target Condition**|**Adaptation Effort**|**System Goal**|**Adaptive Capacity (Buffer)**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|**Stable to Stable**|Step function from stable situation A to stable situation B|A temporary effort, an impulse.|Do not overstrain the system during the adjustment to new conditions.|Buffer needed during a fixed period (treated as a **'project'**).|
|**Stable to Continuous Change**|Switch from stable situation A to a situation of continuous, stable change.|A continuous effort; once started, it is constant in time.|Do not overstrain the system now _and_ in the future.|Buffer will have to be maintained (treated as a **'programme'**).|
|**Stable to Increasing Change**|Switch from stable situation A to a situation of increasing change (of an uncertain magnitude).|Requires continuously growing efforts.|Delay overstraining and prepare for fundamental shifts in the system.|Adaptive capacity and buffer need to increase (must be **'institutionalized'**).|

## Why Resilience for Water-Related Disasters?

Water-related disasters do not just damage physical assets; they fundamentally disrupt critical services and functions necessary for society to operate, such as:

- Flood protection
    
- Drainage
    
- Water supply
    
- Sanitation
    
- Mobility
    
- Emergency response
    
- Ecosystem services
    

## Traditional Risk-Based vs. Resilience-Based Approach

There is a fundamental shift in how systems are managed:

|**Feature**|**Traditional FRM Approach**|**Resilience-Based Approach**|
|---|---|---|
|**Problem Perception**|Changes in the system are predictable.|Changes in the system are uncertain.|
|**Key Objective**|Control changes, maintain stability (problem solving).|Persistence, enhance the capacity to adapt to uncertainties (anticipation).|
|**Governance Perspective**|• Sequential process of planning.<br><br>  <br><br>• Top-down strategy making.<br><br>  <br><br>• Systems of static norms & standards.|• Continuous alignment of content with changing context.<br><br>  <br><br>• Bottom-up initiatives.<br><br>  <br><br>• Systems offering strategic alternatives.|

## Key Takeaways

- Resilience focuses on maintaining function under shocks and uncertain futures.
    
- Resilience includes resistance, coping, recovery, adaptation, and sometimes transformation.
    
- Resilience in water systems is physical, ecological, social, and institutional.
    



# Module 02: Planning Resilience & Adaptation Pathways

## Block B & C: Planning and Coping with Uncertainty

### Strategy vs. Planning

- **Strategy:** Describes _how_ the ends (goals) will be achieved by the means (resources).
    
- **Planning:** The process of thinking about the activities required to achieve a desired goal.
    
    - **Planning components:** Actions, Timeline, Resources, Responsibilities.
        

### Coping with Uncertainty

When developing flood resilience and incorporating uncertainty into planning, there are traditional approaches that have flaws:

- **Strategy 1: Planning for the worst**
    
    - Resource intensive.
        
    - Might have a strong impact (spatial, social).
        
    - Still leaves residual risk.
        
- **Strategy 2: Waiting for scientific certainty/consensus**
    
    - Might be too late for timely readjustment (due to the time needed for the implementation period).
        
    - New insights might NOT actually increase confidence bounds.
        
    - Risk might be increasing rapidly due to anthropogenic (human-caused) changes while waiting.
        

### Adaptive Planning Types

To cope with future uncertainties, planners use different forms of adaptive planning, moving from Robustness to Flexibility:

1. **Assumption-Based Planning:** Sensitivity analysis of “load-bearing” assumptions (a variation on standard sensitivity analysis).
    
2. **Robust Decision Making (RDM):** Identifying policies that deliver under different scenarios.
    
3. **Adaptive Policy Making (APM):** Continuous adjustment.
    
4. **Adaptation Tipping Points (ATP) and Adaptation Pathways (AP):** Identifying exceedance and shifting policies to meet objectives under different scenarios.
    
5. **Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (DAPP):** A combination of APM + ATP + AP.
    

## Block D: Scenario Development

### How to Develop Scenarios

Scenarios are used to explore uncertainty. The initial process to develop them is:

1. **Identify drivers.**
    
2. **Assess their relative uncertainty and impact** on the domain in question (using an impact-uncertainty diagram).
    
3. **Rank and select top drivers.**
    
    - _Example Ranking:_ 1. Trans-boundary water management, 2. Political stability, 3. Rural-urban migration, 4. Climate Change.
        

### Delta Programme 2025: KNMI 23' Scenarios

This is an example of exploring uncertainty using a matrix of four scenarios based on varying degrees of climate impact (Emissions x Climate). These varying conditions (wetter winters, drier summers, extreme events) require flexible policy adaptation.

|**Dry Climate**|**Wetter Climate**|
|---|---|---|
|**High Emissions**|Strong temperature increase and heat. Moderate to extreme increase of drought.|Strong temperature increase and heat. Wet to extremely wet winters. Moderate to extreme increase in summer rainstorms. High sea level rise.|
|**Low Emissions**|Limited temperature increase and heat. Moderate increase of drought. Moderate sea level rise.|Limited temperature increase and heat. Somewhat wetter winters. Moderate increase in summer rainstorms. Moderate sea level rise.|

### The 7-Step Scenario Development Procedure

1. Define the decision problem.
    
2. Identify external drivers.
    
3. Assess drivers by uncertainty and impact.
    
4. Select two critical uncertainties.
    
5. Build a scenario matrix.
    
6. Describe scenario narratives.
    
7. Translate narratives into indicators.
    

## Block E: Developing Persistence (Robustness)

### Robust Decision Making (RDM)

- **Concept:** A robust strategy performs satisfactorily across _many_ plausible futures, rather than optimally in one expected future. It involves developing policies/strategies/measures that are effective under different scenarios.
    
    - **No regret strategies/measures:** Measures that work in _all_ future scenarios.
        
    - **Low regret strategies/measures:** Measures that work in _many_ future scenarios (based on ranking, performance criteria, and assessment frameworks).
        

### RDM Method Overview

|**Attribute**|**Detail**|
|---|---|
|**Central Question**|Which strategy performs acceptably across many plausible futures?|
|**Main Input**|Alternative strategies; scenarios.|
|**Main Output**|Robust, vulnerable, and scenario-specific options.|
|**Best Used For**|Robust, vulnerable, and scenario-specific options.|
|**Main Limitation**|Can become data- and analysis-intensive; "robust" depends on chosen criteria.|

### 4 Domain Approach / 3PA Approach

Robust design requires designing for different types of events (domains):

- Day-to-day events
    
- Design events
    
- Exceedance events
    
- Extreme events
    

### Urban Futures Methodology

- Use scenarios to avoid the problem of specific projections.
    
- Goal: Develop a strategy that will be sound for a variety of plausible futures (a robust strategy).
    

## Block F: Adaptation Tipping Points (ATPs) and Pathways

### Solution: Incorporate Flexibility

To effectively use adaptation pathways, the system/planning requires specific conditions:

- Modularity
    
- Reversibility
    
- Keeping options open
    
- Avoiding lock-in
    
- Signposts and triggers
    
- Institutional flexibility
    

### Adaptation Tipping Points (ATP)

- **Concept:** Identifying exceedance.
    
- **Definition:** An Adaptation Tipping Point (ATP) is that exact point in time when a management strategy or measure will _no longer meet_ the goals/objectives/criteria for a given future scenario.
